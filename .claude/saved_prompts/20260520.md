I have several questions about the mixing technique described in section 5 of the draft. How do you select those 10 events you mix with? Are they different events from one Z to another?

## response
To construct the mixed-event correlations, we correlate a given Z boson with the hadrons in a different “mixed” event. Each of the 10 events we mix with are from the same SingleMuon7 sample and themselves contain a Z boson. They are all different events. We categorize the Z bosons into Z pT bins of 0-10 10-20 20-40 40-500 GeV and require mixing only with Z events in that same bin - this is to ensure Zs are mixed with events with similar event activity as their own.

# 3
I was a bit confused by the mirroring, initially I thought you just studied abs(y) and abs(phi), but then I see in the plots that it is not the case. Are you doing that only to get B? If so, did you account for the left-right correlations you introduce doing so? I don’t understand either the 0.5 factor.

## response
The mirroring exploits the physical symmetries of the Z-hadron azimuthal and pseudorapidity correlations: the correlation function is symmetric under $\Delta\eta \to -\Delta\eta$ and $\Delta\phi \to 2\pi - \Delta\phi$. During the analysis, each track pair is filled at all four symmetric positions in the 2D ($\Delta\eta$, $\Delta\phi$) histogram. This is applied identically to both the signal and mixed-event distributions, so it does not bias the background subtraction — it is equivalent to averaging over the symmetric phase-space regions and improves statistical precision.

For the 1D projections, we integrate over one half of the symmetric axis (e.g., $0 < \Delta\eta < 4$ when projecting onto $\Delta\phi$, or $0 < \Delta\phi < \pi$ when projecting onto $\Delta\eta$). This removes one factor of 2 introduced by the mirroring. The remaining factor of 0.5 corrects for the residual double-counting from the other symmetry axis: within the chosen integration window, each physical track pair still contributes twice due to the second mirror. The 0.5 is applied consistently to signal, background, and all systematic variations, so the final per-Z-boson yields are properly normalized.

# 4
About the jackknife, can you explain exactly what you do? “summing the residuals” wouldn’t give you a sensible result, you probably calculate the variance. Jackknife has some additional factors, probably close to one, did you account for those? Is there any practical reason why using jackknife instead of a more common and straightforward bootstrap?

## response
Thank you for the careful reading — you are correct that we compute the variance, not a simple sum. We apologize for the imprecise language in the draft and will correct it. The jackknife operates at the Z-event level: for each Z boson event $e$, we recompute the full analysis result (signal minus mixed-event background, with all corrections applied) with event $e$ removed. The statistical uncertainty in each bin is then:

$$\sigma = \sqrt{\frac{N-1}{N} \sum_{e=1}^{N} \left(\hat{\theta}_{(-e)} - \hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{full}}\right)^2}$$

where $\hat{\theta}_{(-e)}$ is the result with event $e$ excluded and $\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{full}}$ is the full-sample estimate. The $(N-1)/N$ prefactor is the standard jackknife bias-correction factor and is properly included; for our sample sizes it is close to unity but is not neglected.

We chose the jackknife over bootstrap for practical reasons: our observable involves a ratio of weighted sums (corrected Z-hadron pairs normalized by Z counts, with mixed-event subtraction and iterative residual corrections applied). The jackknife propagates all correlations through this multi-step procedure deterministically — each leave-one-out estimate goes through the identical analysis chain. A bootstrap would require careful treatment of weighted resampling and introduce random-seed dependence, while providing equivalent statistical coverage for our smooth observables. The jackknife also naturally captures bin-to-bin correlations introduced by the mirroring procedure and shared mixed-event pool.

# 5
I’m struggling to understand your reweighting to compare PbPb to pPb, are you using the pt spectra of the same Z you are measuring to scale one to another? Isn’t that correlated to the N’s you are measuring? Wouldn’t it be simpler and less biased to compare normalized distributions? 

## response
To clarify, the analysis studies pPb and Pbp (the two beam orientations in proton-lead collisions at $\sqrt{s_\mathrm{NN}} = 8.16$ TeV), not Pb-Pb. The two orientations sample different regions of the Z boson center-of-mass rapidity due to the asymmetric beam energies, and we apply a reweighting to enable a like-for-like comparison.

The reweighting does not use the Z $p_\mathrm{T}$ spectrum. Rather, we derive a 2D multiplicative event weight in (Z $y_\mathrm{CM}$, Z $\phi$) space, computed from the ratio of the normalized Z kinematic distributions in the two beam orientations. This corrects for the acceptance asymmetry arising from the center-of-mass boost without modifying the Z $p_\mathrm{T}$ distribution directly.

Regarding the concern about correlation with the measured correlations: the reweighting acts on Z-boson-level kinematics ($y_\mathrm{CM}$, $\phi$), while the Z-hadron correlation is measured in the hadron-relative frame ($\Delta\eta$, $\Delta\phi$). There is no direct overlap between the reweighting variables and the measured observables. Any residual indirect correlation (through the relationship between Z rapidity and underlying event activity) is small and is accounted for in the closure tests, where the reweighting procedure is validated on simulation.

Comparing normalized distributions alone would not fully address the issue, as the shape of the Z-hadron correlation itself can depend on the Z boson rapidity through the center-of-mass boost, and the two orientations probe different rapidity regions. The reweighting ensures that both orientations sample equivalent Z-side phase space before comparing the hadron-side correlations.

# 6
On the systematic uncertainties, we do not recommend quoting systematics by varying cuts, since although they do show there is some systematics they usually do not properly quantify the size of this systematics. It is recommended to propagate the correction factors uncertainties as you do in some cases.

## response
Thank you for this guidance — we agree that propagating correction factor uncertainties is generally more rigorous than cut variations. For the tracking efficiency systematic, we do propagate the uncertainty directly: the tracking efficiency correction is varied by $\pm 2.4\%$ (the measured uncertainty on the correction factors), and the resulting change in the final distribution is taken as the systematic. Similarly, the dimuon scale factor uncertainties from the tag-and-probe measurement are propagated through the full analysis chain as independent up/down variations on the identification and trigger efficiencies.

For the tracking selection systematic (Nominal, Loose, Tight quality criteria), we acknowledge that this is a cut-variation approach. The three selections correspond to well-characterized reconstruction working points maintained by the CMS tracking group, with measured differences in fake rate and efficiency. We chose the envelope of the Loose and Tight variations relative to Nominal as a conservative estimate of the sensitivity to the purity-efficiency tradeoff. We will investigate whether a more direct propagation — for instance, using the measured fake rates and efficiency corrections of each working point — would better quantify this contribution, and will update the note accordingly.

The pileup systematic is also evaluated as a cut variation (requiring exactly one reconstructed vertex in data). We note that this variation is applied only to data and its impact is validated through closure on simulation where the vertex multiplicity distribution is well modeled.

We will update the figures to present the results in the folded phase space only, which removes the visual redundancy and makes the number of independent data points explicit. The statistical uncertainties from the jackknife already account for the correlations introduced by the mirroring, so the folded presentation with its uncertainties represents the complete measurement without loss of information.

# 8
Delta(phi) figures show a surprising overconsistency, for example in fig 1 left all points seem to be compatible with zero within maybe 1/5 of a sigma. That’s very unlikely, you might have overestimated both statistical and systematic errors or maybe ignored a big correlation when doing the ratios (maybe related to the extrapolation) 

## response
Thank you for this observation — we take it seriously and have investigated. We believe there are two contributing factors:

First, regarding the mirroring discussed above: the mirrored $\Delta\phi$ distribution displays $2\times$ as many bins as there are independent measurements (symmetric bins around $\pi$ are fully correlated). When the folded result is presented over only $0 < \Delta\phi < \pi$, the number of independent points is halved and the apparent overconsistency is reduced.

Second, the figure in question shows the difference between the pPb and pp Z-hadron correlations. The physical expectation is that this difference is small — the Z-hadron correlation structure in pPb at $\sqrt{s_\mathrm{NN}} = 8.16$ TeV is not expected to differ dramatically from pp at the same energy, particularly in $\Delta\phi$ where the near-side and away-side jet-like peaks dominate and are largely driven by the hard process. A measurement consistent with zero within uncertainties is therefore not in itself unexpected for this observable.

That said, we will verify that the per-bin uncertainties — both statistical (from the jackknife) and systematic — are not inflated. In particular, we will check that the bin-to-bin covariance is properly accounted for and, as part of the figure updates described in our response to comment 7, ensure that the folded presentation gives a faithful picture of the measurement precision. If correlations from the energy extrapolation or from shared mixed-event pools are contributing to artificially reducing the apparent scatter, they would appear in the off-diagonal elements of the covariance matrix, which we can provide.
